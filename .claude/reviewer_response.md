# Response to Reviewer Comments — WISE-APP Methodology

Thank you for these — both are substantive and well-founded, and they sharpen how we present and constrain the tool. On the first point, we agree the report's *language* overreaches in specific, identifiable places, and we will correct it; but we make the case that the RIF simulation is defensible and worth retaining once reframed, particularly given that we observe multiple survey rounds in the same locations with location fixed effects (a repeated cross-section rather than a household panel). On the second, we concede the substance and propose to source policy effects from causal evidence. We take each in turn.

---

## 1. On the UQR / RIF approach

### 1.0 The core mechanism you raise: conditioning on realized consumption

We want to engage this point directly, because it turns on a distinction we did not draw clearly enough in the report.

The mechanism you describe — using a household's realized (post-shock) consumption to place it in a quantile, then estimating a within-quantile weather effect — is a real source of one-directional bias. It is exactly what a conditional quantile regression, or a "run the weather regression within each consumption decile" analysis, would suffer from. **But it is not what the RIF/UQR *estimation* does.** The RIF coefficient β_τ is a partial effect on an *unconditional* population quantile q_τ (the τ-th percentile of the whole sample's consumption), obtained by OLS of the recentered influence function on weather. No household is sorted into a bin by its own consumption, and no within-bin effect is estimated; q_τ is a fixed feature of the population distribution (Firpo, Fortin & Lemieux 2009).

To be precise about what is and is not correct in your critique: realized, post-weather consumption *does* enter the estimation — the RIF transform uses each household's welfare both to locate the population cutoff q_τ and to evaluate the indicator 1(y ≤ q_τ). The question is whether that use reproduces the one-directional bias, and it does not. The bias requires that the variable used to *group* a unit be the same shock whose effect is then estimated *within* that group — the entanglement of conditioning and treatment at the unit level that afflicts CQR and decile-split regressions. RIF does not condition: it regresses a transform of welfare on weather across the *whole* sample, and β_τ is identified from the covariance between weather and the RIF-transformed outcome over all households, using within-location weather variation over time (given our fixed effects). A household that a bad weather draw pushed below q_τ enters that covariance in a way that *correctly* registers that the shock lowered the τ-th percentile — which is the causal object of interest, not an artifact.

The consequence is that the estimator is **not sign-constrained**, which is the cleanest test of the "mechanically finds the poor hurt most" claim. If weather in truth hurt every household by the same proportion, the whole distribution shifts left uniformly, every q_τ moves by the same amount, and RIF returns a *flat* β_τ — it does not manufacture a steeper low-τ effect. If weather instead compressed the distribution from the top, RIF returns a *top-heavy* β_τ. The method reports whichever pattern the data show; the "always finds the poor hurt most" property belongs to the decile-split / conditional estimator, not to UQR.

There is one honest estimation-level caveat, distinct from the bias you raise. Because RIF estimates the movement of *anonymous* quantiles (the position q_τ, not a fixed set of households), reading a downward shift in q_10 as "the same poor households lost X" assumes the composition of the lower tail is stable — that weather does not cause large reranking across the distribution. RIF correctly estimates how the *percentile* moves regardless; what it cannot certify is that the households at that percentile before and after are the same ones. This is the standard unconditional-quantile (anonymity) caveat, and post-weather consumption is why it applies. It is adjacent to, but weaker than, the mechanical bias: it limits the household-level reading, not the distributional one — which is exactly why our fix is to report the distributional object and drop the household-level attribution.

Where your critique **does** bind is our *simulation and interpretation*, not the estimation. In assigning each household a position τ_i from its baseline (realized) consumption, reading off β at that position, and describing the result as "poorer households are hit hardest," we reintroduced precisely the conditioning-on-outcome problem you identify — because we do not observe pre-shock rank. We accept this fully. Our fix distinguishes two things that both get called "the household-level assignment." We *keep* the mechanical operation — assigning each household a position τ_i and reading its weather sensitivity off the estimated gradient — because it is what lets the tool simulate targeted policy and aggregate up to distributional metrics (see §1.4c), and reframe it as reading off an estimated distributional gradient rather than a causal rank claim. We *drop* the causal interpretation — the claim that a household is more vulnerable *because* it is structurally poor — which the data do not identify. Concretely: (i) we present the RIF results as a distributional statement about how weather reshapes the consumption distribution (the object the estimation actually identifies), and (ii) we stop reading the household-level assignment as a claim about which structurally-poor households are most vulnerable, while retaining it as the interpolation device the simulation needs. This keeps the part of the analysis that is free of the bias you describe and removes the part that is not.

### 1.1 Where we agree — and will change the report

Two passages currently make a household-level causal claim our data do not identify, and these are the right target of your critique:

- The statement that a household's weather sensitivity is *"fundamentally determined by its pre-shock baseline welfare rank."*
- The passage attributing quantile position to household-level adaptive capacity — *"poorer households often face higher exposure and possess significantly fewer adaptive capacities (e.g., savings, insurance)... making their welfare much more sensitive."*

With a repeated cross-section (we have location, not household, fixed effects; see footnote 5) we cannot separate "structurally poor households are more vulnerable" from "households whose consumption fell now sit lower in the distribution." We will rewrite both passages, and remove the claim that the RIF simulation *"yields much more accurate estimates"* of poverty and inequality — a claim we cannot demonstrate at the household level and which is vulnerable to precisely the one-directional bias identified in §1.0. (It survives, however, as an *aggregate-distributional* claim; see §1.4b.)

### 1.2 The reframe — from household trait to estimated distributional gradient

The β_τ curve is a valid Firpo–Fortin–Lemieux object: it describes how the marginal effect of weather on the *unconditional* τ-th quantile varies *across strata of the consumption distribution*. Reframed this way, the RIF simulation makes a **distributional-strata** statement — "weather sensitivity differs across positions in the population consumption distribution, and we apply the empirically estimated gradient at each household's decile" — not a claim about the essential vulnerability of particular households. This is what the math actually does, and it drops the unidentified causal story about savings and insurance.

### 1.3 Why this is defensible for the changes we simulate — not merely assumed

- **Multiple rounds and location fixed effects make the strata observable, not assumed.** Because we observe the consumption distribution in the same locations across survey waves (60 surveys, 21 countries, 2M+ observations), decile membership is not a single-snapshot artifact — we can report the *stratum-level* round-to-round rank stability of the distribution as evidence for the decile stability the simulation relies on. This is stratum-level, not household-level tracking, since GMD gives us a repeated cross-section rather than a household panel.
- **The assumption's content is second-order in the effect size.** Rank-dependence does real work in the simulation only through the repositioning channel, δ_res1 = [β_w(τ_post) − β_w(τ_pre)] · w, which fires in proportion to how far a policy or shock moves a household to a *new* decile. For a marginal expansion or a single-season anomaly, τ_post ≈ τ_pre, the bracket collapses, and the result is first-order insensitive to whether the household "truly belongs" at that rank. The assumption is load-bearing only for *large* shifts that reshuffle households across deciles — exactly where we would flag extrapolation regardless of method.
- **We will bound it per run.** For each simulation we propose to report the share of households whose τ moves by more than one decile, and flag large-movement runs as out-of-support. This turns the objection into a diagnostic the tool checks.

### 1.4 Why it adds value over the linear model — and why we keep it

**In brief:** both engines are built on post-shock consumption, so OLS is not a cleaner benchmark — it simply discards the distributional information RIF uses. Using that information gives more accurate tail-sensitive aggregates (poverty, gap, Gini) and lets us model targeted, resilience-building policy that OLS zeroes out by construction; the cost is a rank-stability assumption that is mild for the marginal changes the tool simulates and flagged by a diagnostic otherwise. The three subsections below develop this: expressiveness (§1.4), aggregate accuracy (§1.4b), and policy capability (§1.4c).

OLS mathematically enforces a single weather slope across the whole distribution, so any distributional heterogeneity must be pre-specified by the analyst as weather × wealth interactions — and if the relevant interaction is omitted, OLS silently returns zero heterogeneity. RIF estimates the distributional profile directly without that guesswork. For a tool whose central question is *how climate risk is distributed across the population*, a method that can represent a non-uniform shift of the lower tail is strictly more expressive than one that assumes a uniform shift. We therefore propose to **retain the RIF simulation, reframed as a distributional-strata tool**, rather than remove it.

### 1.4b Why the UQR *simulation* is reasonable — and better than OLS for the aggregates we report

**First, the premise that OLS is the clean benchmark is not right — OLS is built on post-shock consumption too.** The concern that "we only observe realized, post-shock consumption" applies to the linear engine verbatim. OLS regresses *post-shock* log consumption on weather; its counterfactual prediction is y_baseline + β·Δw, added to the *same* post-shock baseline level RIF uses; and its poverty, gap, and Gini figures are computed off that same post-shock distribution. OLS does not observe pre-shock rank either — it simply does not *use* rank at all. But declining to use the distributional position is not the same as being immune to the problem; it is discarding real information. So the choice is not "RIF (contaminated) vs. OLS (clean)." Both rest on the same observed outcome. The real question is whether it is better to use the distributional structure in that outcome or to throw it away.

Given that, the simulation must assign each household a weather sensitivity, and neither engine observes the true structural value — so this is a choice between two assignment rules, not correct-vs-biased. OLS assigns the single conditional-mean slope to every household; it does not escape the problem, it *assumes* the homogeneity that the data reject. The UQR simulation instead applies the slope estimated at each household's position on the welfare distribution, β_w(τ_i).

Although τ_i is measured post-shock, the assignment is an *interpolation onto an unbiased distributional object* (the β_w(τ) curve from §1.0), not a causal rank claim: a household at a given welfare level is handed the sensitivity the data show at that level. Its payoff is at exactly the level the tool reports. Poverty headcount, poverty gap, and Gini responses depend on the *shape* of the distributional shift, not on any individual household. Applying position-specific slopes reproduces the estimated non-uniform response; OLS's uniform slope shifts the distribution roughly rigidly and therefore mismeasures the very tail movements these metrics capture. So the household-level labeling can be imperfect while the **aggregate distributional response is more accurate than OLS** — because the object the simulation is built from is the valid distributional one.

Two further points narrow the residual concern. First, for weather-only scenarios (the tool's core use case) τ_i is fixed and the simulation is just β_w(τ_i)·Δw — the endogeneity of τ_i affects nothing, because no repositioning occurs. Second, the case where OLS could genuinely be safer is *large reranking* (the household at τ_i post-shock is not the one that was there pre-shock), which harms the household attribution but not the aggregate. This is the same movement as the across-decile shift discussed in §1.3, so the same effect-size logic bounds it: for the marginal policy changes the tool simulates, τ_post ≈ τ_pre and reranking is negligible, so the household-attribution weakness conceded here is small in that regime. The one exception is a genuinely *large* weather draw (a severe drought is not marginal), where reranking from the weather realisation itself need not be small — but that is precisely the out-of-support case the decile-movement diagnostic (§1.3) is designed to flag, and it degrades the household reading, not the distributional aggregate the tool reports.

### 1.4c Why the RIF assignment is the right fit for the tool's policy simulations

Beyond the aggregate-accuracy argument, the RIF assignment rule is what lets the tool do what it is for — simulate *targeted* policies defined on household characteristics — coherently.

- **Targeting and effect are consistent under RIF.** The policy module targets households by observed traits (poverty-targeted transfers, eligibility thresholds). Under OLS, once a household is targeted, the welfare effect applied to it is the same constant slope wherever it sits, so the tool cannot represent that the targeted (poorer) households also face a different weather gradient — it can represent *who* receives the policy but not that *their* vulnerability differs. Under RIF, targeting the bottom of the distribution and applying the weather sensitivity estimated at the bottom are the same distributional object, so "transfer to the poorest, who also have the steepest weather gradient" is one coherent statement rather than two hand-coded pieces.
- **The resilience-via-welfare channel is a capability OLS lacks by construction, not a nuisance term.** For OLS the repositioning channel is *identically zero* (see method_lin.md §2.7): lifting a household's welfare cannot reduce its modelled weather sensitivity unless the analyst has pre-specified a cash×weather interaction. But "can this intervention make households less climate-vulnerable by making them less poor" is the central question a resilience tool exists to answer. OLS answers it with a structural zero — certainly wrong if any resilience-via-welfare effect exists. RIF answers it with an estimate from the fitted gradient, under the stated rank-stability assumption. An estimate under a mild, diagnosed assumption is more useful than a zero that is known to be false.

**A necessary honesty about large targeted transfers.** We want to be explicit about where this is strongest and weakest, using the flagship case of a transfer targeted to the bottom 40%. Such a transfer has two channels, and they do not share the same footing:

- The **direct effect** — adding the cash to observed welfare, δ_sp = log(exp(y) + cash) − y — is *always valid*. It does not use the RIF gradient or rank-invariance at all; it simply targets households where they actually sit in the observed distribution, which is what a real program does. This channel is on the same clean footing as OLS.
- The **repositioning effect** — moving the household from τ_pre to τ_post and applying the lower weather sensitivity β_w(τ_post) — is where an extrapolation enters, and a bottom-40% transfer is precisely the case that stresses it, because it is *designed* to move households across deciles (so τ_post ≠ τ_pre by construction). Applying β_w(τ_post) then assumes a formerly-poor household that received a transfer takes on the weather vulnerability of a household that was *originally* at that higher welfare level. This is the rank-invariance assumption in its most demanding form: originally-poor households may differ in unobserved ways (asset quality, geography, networks) that the welfare level alone does not capture (this is the caveat in method_rif.md §2.7c).

We are not claiming the repositioning estimate is unbiased for large targeted transfers — it is an extrapolation, and we will label it as such and surface it with the decile-movement diagnostic. But the relevant comparison is still against OLS, which sets this channel to exactly zero — i.e., asserts that a bottom-40% transfer does *nothing* to reduce climate vulnerability beyond the cash itself. Given that any real resilience-via-welfare effect makes zero the wrong answer, a transparent extrapolation from the empirically estimated gradient — flagged, bounded, and reported alongside the always-valid direct effect — is more informative than a structural zero, provided it is presented as a scenario estimate rather than a point prediction.

Stated plainly, the honest hierarchy is not "OLS correct, RIF risky." Both simulate off post-shock consumption; OLS copes by assuming a homogeneity the data reject and zeroing the resilience channel by fiat, whereas RIF uses the estimated distributional gradient — unbiased as a distributional object, more accurate on the tail-sensitive aggregates the tool reports, and able to represent targeted policy and resilience-via-welfare. The price is the rank-stability assumption, which is second-order for the marginal changes the tool simulates and flagged by diagnostics for the large ones. RIF is therefore OLS's information *used* rather than *discarded*, at the cost of an assumption that is mild in the tool's operating regime.

### 1.5 Proposed resolution (report edits and diagnostics, not removal)

1. Rewrite the two flagged passages and the "more accurate" claim; recast rank-dependence as an estimated distributional gradient across strata, not a household trait, and preserve the accuracy claim only as an aggregate-distributional statement.
2. State the rank-stability assumption explicitly as a controlled approximation valid for marginal changes.
3. Add two diagnostics: per-run decile-movement share, and stratum-level round-to-round rank stability.
4. Add a footnote to the Quantile-regression subsection making the unconditional-vs-conditional (estimation-vs-simulation) distinction explicit in the text itself, and noting the anonymity caveat — that RIF identifies the movement of population quantiles, not of a fixed set of households, so the distributional reading is licensed but the household-level one is not.
5. Note the Abadie–Chingos–West split-sample estimators as a future refinement contingent on a time-invariant welfare proxy, not a prerequisite.

## 2. On policy simulations using observational coefficients (infrastructure, education)

**We concede the substance.** The weather coefficient is plausibly causal within-panel; the infrastructure and education coefficients are associational and should not be read as the effect of *expanding* access. Flipping a covariate and pricing the change at its fitted β measures the cross-sectional consumption gap between households that do and do not currently hold the asset — absorbing selection and unobservables — not the causal effect of the intervention.

**The panel design is exactly why this asymmetry is principled, not a double standard.** The report's own identification argument is that the *timing* of weather deviations is plausibly exogenous *conditional on location fixed effects* (Dell/Auffhammer) — this holds for weather because it is identified off within-location variation over time. The policy coefficients are identified largely off *cross-sectional* variation in access, which location fixed effects cannot clean, because access is correlated with the traits those fixed effects absorb or leave confounded. The same design that *validates* the weather channel is what *exposes* the policy coefficients as associational. We will state this explicitly rather than leaving the two on equal footing.

**Proposed changes:**

1. Source policy-lever effects from causal evidence — a context-specific impact evaluation or a systematic-review ATE, entered with source and confidence interval, applied *instead of* the fitted β for that lever. The regression stays authoritative only for the weather channel.
2. Separate the two visibly in the UI and report: "weather impacts — estimated from your data; policy impacts — supplied from causal evidence."
3. On resilience: where a credible weather-interacted treatment effect exists, allow the user to enter it; otherwise disable the resilience channel rather than impute it from an observational interaction term.

One point in both our favours: the RIF repositioning channel (§1) does *not* use the observational policy β — it operates through movement along the estimated weather gradient. So after we swap observational policy coefficients for causal ones, the RIF simulation's distinctive resilience mechanism remains intact and correctly sourced.

We believe these changes — targeted report rewrites, two new diagnostics, and externally-sourced policy effects — address the substance of both comments while retaining what the RIF approach uniquely contributes. We would welcome your view on whether this framing is one you would be comfortable with in the report.
